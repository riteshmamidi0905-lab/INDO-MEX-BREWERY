## 1. Executive vision

**The thesis.** Most "fusion" fails because it mixes *flavors*. This brewery should mix *structures*: the logic by which two food cultures build balance. India and Mexico are unusually compatible on structure. Both are chile cultures built on layered heat, acid (lime, tamarind, kokum), toasted spice and seed, slow-cooked meat, flatbreads (roti/paratha/tortilla), fresh herb (cilantro) and ferment. Both cook on a flat hot griddle (tawa / comal) `[UNCITED]`. Both have a corn-and-wheat bread tradition. Both cultures treat the sauce as the soul of the dish. That shared grammar is why this can be a real cuisine rather than a gimmick.

**The third layer.** Austin supplies the container: a serious craft-beer city, live music, patios, limestone, live oak, heat. The brewery should feel *Texan in its bones* and *non-Texan in its details*.

**The one idea to build everything on.** *The courtyard.* The Indian haveli/angan and the Mexican hacienda/patio are both buildings organized around an open court. We put the **brewhouse at the center of the court**. The tanks are the heart; dining, bar and patio are arranged around them. That single move gives us architecture, brand story, customer journey and a recognizable silhouette. (Detail in chapter 8.)

**The beer promise.** Prove mastery first. A world-class hefeweizen and a world-class Mexican-style lager before any spice. Regional beers come after the Classics earn trust.

**Business reality.** A single-location brewpub in Austin is a realistic first goal. National distribution is a separate, later licensing and capital question `[PRO]`. The vision should not be diluted to chase scale early.

## 2. Brand philosophy

1. **Structure over souvenir.** No decorative clichés (no paisley wallpaper next to papel picado, no sombreros, no maharaja cartoons). Borrow *principles*: lattice screens that filter light, thick plaster and stone that cool, water, brass and copper that age, tile as pattern, fire as the center.
2. **Beer is a craft first, a concept second.** Every regional beer must pass a blind test: "Would I order this if it had a plain name?"
3. **Respect through specificity.** Name the *region*, the *ingredient*, the *technique*, not "exotic". Credit sources and cooks. Hire from both cultures.
4. **Say yes to everyone.** The room should be natural to a craft-beer regular from Round Rock, an Indian-American family, and a Mexican-American couple on a first date. Nobody should feel like the "theme" is about someone else.
5. **Warmth, not spectacle.** Theater only if it improves the beer or the table. No dry ice, no smoke domes, no unsafe fire.
6. **Recognizable without the logo.** Our four "signatures": the *glass*, the *tank-at-the-center* silhouette, the *char-and-color plate* and the *can* (pattern system). Chapter 9 defines these.

## Skeptical advisor's top risks (read these first)
1. **"Indo Mex" as a name is descriptive and generic.** It may be hard to protect and may read as a novelty. A distinct parent brand with "Indo-Mex" as the descriptor is probably stronger (chapter 9). `[PRO]` trademark.
2. **Menu sprawl.** 25 dishes is a concept library, not an opening menu. An opening menu should be 12-16 dishes with shared prep.
3. **Spice in beer is a flavor-stability and consistency problem.** Ingredients like chile and mango behave unpredictably in fermentation. Regional beers need pilot proof; some will be rejected.
4. **Capital intensity.** A visible brewhouse, kitchen and large patio is expensive. Capacity, build-out cost and Austin rents are unresearched `[PRO]`.
5. **Texas alcohol law shapes the business model.** Brewpub vs. brewery licensing determines whether you can sell packaged beer, distribute, or serve guest beer. Needs an attorney before site or equipment decisions `[PRO]`.
6. **Cultural sensitivity.** Words, religious imagery and "royalty" tropes can land badly. Review by people from the cultures involved before finalizing names `[PRO]`.
