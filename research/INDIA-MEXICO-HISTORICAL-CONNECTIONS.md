# India × Mexico: Historical Connections (research record)

Purpose: decide what the brewery may truthfully say about connections. Status labels come from `HISTORICAL-CLAIMS-AUDIT.md`. **If there is no established connection we say so.** Nothing here is exhibit text.

## 1. Connections with solid support
**1.1 The chile (strongest).** Chile peppers were domesticated in Mexico `[VERIFIED S-59]`. They reached India in the 16th century via Portuguese-era trade `[SUPPORTED S-45a,b]`; before then, India's heat came from black and long pepper. Result: today's Indian and Mexican cuisines both rest on a plant that Mexico gave the world. *Caution:* "Mexico gave India its fire" is a slogan, not a finding; chile reached India through Europe and likely Brazil, not directly from Mexico.
**1.2 The Pacific trade.** From 1565 to 1815 the Manila galleon connected the Philippines to Acapulco; silk, porcelain, spices, ivory and Indian cotton moved east, silver moved west `[VERIFIED S-61, S-62]`. People were also carried: enslaved persons from the Indian subcontinent and Southeast Asia reached New Spain, classed as *chinos* and later legally as Indians `[VERIFIED S-60, details need the book]`. This is a difficult history and must be told plainly.
**1.3 Twentieth-century diaspora.** Punjabi men and Mexican and Mexican-American women formed families in California from 1916 `[VERIFIED S-36]`. This is a human story with living descendants; use only with their communities' consultation `[PRO]`.

## 3. Things that look connected but are not (say "no established connection")
- **Tandoor and comal:** both ancient clay-based cooking; independent.
- **Maize and millet:** parallel foundational grains.
- **Black pottery, terrazzo, volcanic stone:** design affinities only.
- **Al pastor and Indian food:** al pastor descends from Lebanese shawarma `[VERIFIED S-37]`, not from India; use only as proof that Mexican cuisine absorbs migrant techniques.
- **Fermented drinks** (apong, tesgüino): parallel inventions.

## 4. What the brewery can honestly say (draft principles)
1. India and Mexico are two very different civilizations with long independent food histories.
2. A handful of real crossings exist (chile, the Pacific trade, the diaspora); we show them with sources.
3. Where we do not know, we say so.
4. The cuisine we cook is a *contemporary* conversation, not a historical reconstruction.
